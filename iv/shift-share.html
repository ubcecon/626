This lecture covers shift-share (Bartik) instrumental variables, which have become one of the most widely used quasi-experimental designs in applied microeconomics—especially in labor, trade, urban, and public economics.

A good starting point for students is Borusyak, Hull, and Jaravel (2025) in the Journal of Economic Perspectives, which distills a decade of rapid methodological progress into a practical checklist. The deeper theoretical foundations come from three landmark papers: Goldsmith-Pinkham, Sorkin, and Swift (2020, AER) on share-based identification, Borusyak, Hull, and Jaravel (2022, REStud) on shock-based identification, and Adão, Kolesár, and Morales (2019, QJE) on inference. We will also cover two crucial extensions: Borusyak and Hull (2023, Econometrica) on recentering when exposure is nonrandom, and Jaeger, Ruist, and Stuhler (2018) on dynamic confounding in immigration settings.

Motivation & Setup

What Is a Shift-Share Instrument?

  • Units \(\ell = 1, \dots, L\) (e.g., commuting zones, cities, firms)
  • Sectors / groups \(k = 1, \dots, K\) (e.g., industries, origin countries)
  • Model of interest: \[ Y_\ell = \beta X_\ell + W_\ell' \gamma + u_\ell \]
  • Shift-share (Bartik) instrument: \[ Z_\ell = \sum_{k=1}^K s_{\ell k} g_k \]
    • \(s_{\ell k} \ge 0\): shares measuring unit \(\ell\)’s exposure to sector \(k\) (often pre-determined)
    • \(g_k\): shifts (aggregate shocks) affecting sector \(k\)

Why do researchers construct shift-share instruments? Often we want to estimate the effect of a local economic variable \(X_\ell\) (such as local import competition, local immigrant inflows, or local labor demand growth) on a local outcome \(Y_\ell\) (such as wages or employment). Direct OLS of \(Y_\ell\) on \(X_\ell\) is confounded by local demand or productivity shocks in \(u_\ell\).

The shift-share instrument \(Z_\ell = \sum_k s_{\ell k} g_k\) combines two ingredients: local exposure shares \(s_{\ell k}\) (how important sector \(k\) is in region \(\ell\) at baseline) and aggregate shifts \(g_k\) (national or global shocks to sector \(k\)). Originally popularized by Bartik (1991) and Blanchard and Katz (1992) to instrument for local labor demand, the design is now ubiquitous across applied fields.

Emphasize right away that \(Z_\ell\) is a single instrument built from \(K\) shares and \(K\) shocks. A major theme of the recent literature is that either the shares \(s_{\ell k}\) or the shocks \(g_k\) can justify exogeneity—and which one you rely on changes everything about diagnostics, controls, and standard errors.

Empirical Example 1: The China Shock

  • Autor et al. (2013) study the local labor market effects of Chinese import competition in the U.S. (1990–2007)
  • Units \(\ell\): \(722\) U.S. commuting zones; sectors \(k\): \(\approx 400\) manufacturing industries
  • Outcome \(Y_\ell\): decadal change in manufacturing employment share or wages
  • Endogenous \(X_\ell\): local growth in Chinese imports per worker
  • Shift-share instrument: \[ Z_\ell = \sum_{k=1}^K s_{\ell k} g_k \]
    • \(s_{\ell k}\): commuting zone \(\ell\)’s lagged share of employment in industry \(k\)
    • \(g_k\): growth in Chinese exports to other high-income countries in industry \(k\)

Autor, Dorn, and Hanson (2013, AER) is the canonical modern shift-share application and the running example in Borusyak, Hull, and Jaravel (2022, 2025) and Adão, Kolesár, and Morales (2019).

Why is \(X_\ell\) endogenous? Actual U.S. imports from China in a commuting zone reflect both Chinese supply shocks (productivity growth, WTO accession) and U.S.-specific demand shocks in \(u_\ell\). To isolate the Chinese supply component, ADH instrument with \(Z_\ell\), replacing U.S. industry import growth with Chinese import growth to eight other high-income countries (\(g_k\)), weighted by each commuting zone’s lagged industry employment shares (\(s_{\ell k}\)).

Notice a key feature here: the shares \(s_{\ell k}\) do not sum to one across manufacturing industries! The sum \(S_\ell = \sum_k s_{\ell k}\) is the total manufacturing employment share in commuting zone \(\ell\), which varies widely across the U.S. We will come back to this “incomplete shares” issue—it turns out to be a major source of omitted variable bias if ignored. Also notice how natural it is to think of identification here as coming from the \(K \approx 400\) industry-level Chinese supply shocks \(g_k\), rather than assuming initial manufacturing specialization \(s_{\ell k}\) is randomly assigned across commuting zones.

Empirical Example 2: Immigration & Local Labor Markets

  • Card (2001) studies how immigrant inflows affect native wages and employment across U.S. cities
  • Units \(\ell\): metropolitan areas (cities \(\times\) skill/occupation cells); groups \(k\): origin countries
  • Outcome \(Y_\ell\): native wage or employment rate
  • Endogenous \(X_\ell\): local inflow of new immigrants
  • Shift-share instrument: \[ Z_\ell = \sum_{k=1}^K s_{\ell k} g_k \]
    • \(s_{\ell k}\): historical share of immigrants from country \(k\) living in city \(\ell\) (e.g., 1970 or 1980)
    • \(g_k\): total national inflow of new immigrants from country \(k\)

Card (2001, JoLE) is the second canonical example—often called the “past-settlement” or “ethnic enclave” instrument. Immigrants do not choose cities randomly; they move to cities with booming labor markets, creating positive omitted variable bias in OLS.

Card’s instrument uses the fact that new immigrants tend to settle where earlier compatriots already live. So if a city had 20% of Mexican immigrants in 1980 (\(s_{\ell k} = 0.20\)) and \(g_k\) Mexican immigrants arrive nationally in the 1990s, the predicted local inflow is \(0.20 \cdot g_k\), summed across origin countries \(k\).

Notice how different this example is from the China shock! First, the number of origin countries \(K\) with large flows is often relatively small (e.g., Mexico, Philippines, India, China), so shock-based asymptotics may be fragile. Second, historical settlement shares \(s_{\ell k}\) are very persistent over time, and national inflows \(g_{kt}\) are strongly serially correlated across decades—which leads directly to the Jaeger, Ruist, and Stuhler (2018) critique about conflating short-run and long-run effects.

Identification

Two Paths to Identification

  • After partialling out controls \(W_\ell\), the IV estimand requires \[ \Er\left[\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^L Z_\ell u_\ell\right] = 0 \]
  • Substitute \(Z_\ell = \sum_{k=1}^K s_{\ell k} g_k\) and swap the order of summation: \[ \frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^L Z_\ell u_\ell = \frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^L \left(\sum_{k=1}^K s_{\ell k} g_k\right) u_\ell = \sum_{k=1}^K g_k \underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^L s_{\ell k} u_\ell\right)}_{\text{exposure-weighted residual}} \]
  • Two distinct ways this expectation can be zero:
    1. Exogenous shares (Goldsmith-Pinkham et al. (2020)): \(\Er[s_{\ell k} u_\ell] = 0\) for each \(k\)
    2. Exogenous shocks (Borusyak et al. (2022)): \(\Er[g_k \bar{u}_k] = 0\) across many \(k\)

This single algebraic identity—swapping the sum over regions \(\ell\) and sectors \(k\)—is the heart of the entire shift-share literature.

Look at the right-hand side: \(\sum_{k=1}^K g_k \left(\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^L s_{\ell k} u_\ell\right)\). For this to have mean zero: - Path 1 (Goldsmith-Pinkham, Sorkin, & Swift 2020) assumes that for each sector \(k\), the shares \(s_{\ell k}\) are uncorrelated with the regional error \(u_\ell\): \(\Er[s_{\ell k} u_\ell] = 0\). In this view, the shocks \(g_k\) are just fixed weights that combine \(K\) valid instruments \((s_{\ell 1}, \dots, s_{\ell K})\) into one scalar instrument \(Z_\ell\). - Path 2 (Borusyak, Hull, & Jaravel 2022) allows the shares \(s_{\ell k}\) to be arbitrarily correlated with \(u_\ell\)! Instead, it assumes that the shocks \(g_k\) are quasi-randomly assigned across a large number of sectors \(K\), so that \(g_k\) is uncorrelated with the sector-level average residual \(\bar{u}_k\).

Students often ask: “Do both have to hold?” No! Only one needs to hold, and in most applications one is far more plausible than the other. For instance, in Autor-Dorn-Hanson, nobody believes a commuting zone’s manufacturing specialization is unrelated to local wage trends (\(\Er[s_{\ell k} u_\ell] \ne 0\)), so Path 2 (exogenous Chinese supply shocks) is the natural justification. Conversely, if \(K\) is small (say, 5 origin countries in an immigration study), Path 2 has almost no power across shocks, so you are implicitly relying on Path 1 (exogenous shares).

Path 1: Exogenous Shares (Goldsmith-Pinkham et al. (2020))

  • Assumption: \(\Er[u_\ell \mid s_{\ell 1}, \dots, s_{\ell K}, W_\ell] = 0\) (as \(L \to \infty\), with \(K\) fixed)
    • Effectively, the shares \(s_{\ell 1}, \dots, s_{\ell K}\) are \(K\) separate valid instruments
    • In difference / panel specifications, this is a parallel trends assumption: regions with different initial shares would have evolved similarly absent the shocks
  • Rotemberg weights decomposition: \[ \hat{\beta}_{SSIV} = \sum_{k=1}^K \hat{\alpha}_k \hat{\beta}_k, \qquad \sum_{k=1}^K \hat{\alpha}_k = 1 \]
    • \(\hat{\beta}_k\): just-identified IV estimate using only share \(s_{\ell k}\) as the instrument
    • \(\hat{\alpha}_k\): Rotemberg weight measuring how much sector \(k\) contributes to \(\hat{\beta}_{SSIV}\)

Goldsmith-Pinkham, Sorkin, and Swift (2020) show that when you treat the shocks \(g_k\) as fixed, using the single shift-share instrument \(Z_\ell\) is numerically identical to a GMM / 2SLS estimator that uses the vector of all \(K\) shares \((s_{\ell 1}, \dots, s_{\ell K})\) as instruments with a specific weight matrix given by \(g_k\).

More usefully, they show that \(\hat{\beta}_{SSIV}\) can be decomposed into a weighted average of \(K\) just-identified IV estimates \(\hat{\beta}_k\) (each using a single share \(s_{\ell k}\) as the instrument). The weights \(\hat{\alpha}_k\) (named after Rotemberg 1983) sum to 1, though some can be negative! What is the intuition? If \(\hat{\alpha}_k\) is large for industry \(k\) (say, electronic computers or apparel), that means variation in that specific industry’s share is driving most of your identification. You can then inspect the top-weighted industries directly: are regions with high initial employment in those industries different on observables or pre-trends? You can also run overidentification tests (e.g., Hansen’s \(J\) test) across the shares, though with the caveat that if treatment effects are heterogeneous (\(\beta_\ell\) varies across regions), \(\hat{\beta}_k\) can differ across \(k\) even when all shares are valid.

Path 2: Exogenous Shocks (Borusyak et al. (2022))

  • Assumption: Shocks \(g_k\) are quasi-randomly assigned across many independent (or clustered) sectors \(K\):
    1. Quasi-random shocks: \(\Er[g_k \mid \bar{u}_k, s_k, q_k] = q_k' \mu\) for some controls \(q_k\)
    2. Many independent shocks: \(\sum_{k=1}^K s_k^2 \to 0\), where \(s_k = \frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^L s_{\ell k}\) is average exposure to sector \(k\)
  • Shock-level equivalence: \(\hat{\beta}_{SSIV}\) equals a shock-level IV regression weighted by \(s_k\):
    • Regress exposure-weighted outcome \(\bar{Y}_k\) on exposure-weighted treatment \(\bar{X}_k\), instrumented by \(g_k\), with weights \(s_k\)
    • Here \(\bar{Y}_k = \frac{\sum_\ell s_{\ell k} Y_\ell^\perp}{\sum_\ell s_{\ell k}}\) (similarly \(\bar{X}_k\)), where \(Y_\ell^\perp, X_\ell^\perp\) have controls \(W_\ell\) partialled out

Borusyak, Hull, and Jaravel (2022) flip the perspective completely. Suppose \(s_{\ell k}\) is endogenous—say, regions with lots of manufacturing also have lower educational attainment or different secular trends. Can \(Z_\ell\) still be valid?

Yes! Look back at \(\sum_k g_k \left(\frac{1}{L}\sum_\ell s_{\ell k} u_\ell\right)\). Define \(s_k = \frac{1}{L}\sum_\ell s_{\ell k}\) (total exposure to sector \(k\)) and \(\bar{u}_k = \frac{\sum_\ell s_{\ell k} u_\ell}{\sum_\ell s_{\ell k}}\) (the exposure-weighted average residual in sector \(k\)). Then the orthogonality condition is simply \(\sum_{k=1}^K s_k g_k \bar{u}_k \approx 0\). If \(g_k\) is mean-independent of \(\bar{u}_k\) (conditional on shock-level controls \(q_k\) and exposure \(s_k\)), and no single sector dominates total exposure (so the Herfindahl index \(\sum_k s_k^2 \to 0\), meaning the effective number of shocks \(1/\sum_k s_k^2\) is large), then by a law of large numbers across sectors \(k\), \(\hat{\beta}_{SSIV}\) is consistent!

The shock-level equivalence theorem is super practical: instead of doing all your diagnostics at the region level, you can aggregate the data to the shock level (e.g., using ssaggregate in Stata/R), run shock-level balance checks, visualize the shock-level first stage and reduced form, and cluster standard errors at the shock level.

Pitfalls

Pitfall 1: Incomplete Shares

  • Often \(\sum_{k=1}^K s_{\ell k} = S_\ell \neq 1\) (e.g., total manufacturing share in Autor et al. (2013), or total foreign-born share in Card (2001))
  • Suppose shocks have nonzero mean \(\Er[g_k \mid s] = \mu \neq 0\): \[ Z_\ell = \sum_{k=1}^K s_{\ell k} g_k = \mu S_\ell + \sum_{k=1}^K s_{\ell k}(g_k - \mu) \]
  • Even if \(g_k - \mu\) is purely random, \(Z_\ell\) is correlated with \(S_\ell\)!
  • If \(S_\ell\) correlates with \(u_\ell\) (e.g., manufacturing hubs have different wage trends), \(\hat{\beta}_{SSIV}\) is biased
  • Fix: Include \(S_\ell\) (interacted with period fixed effects in panels) in \(W_\ell\)

This is one of the most important practical discoveries in Borusyak, Hull, and Jaravel (2022). In Autor, Dorn, and Hanson (2013), \(s_{\ell k}\) is the share of total commuting-zone employment in manufacturing industry \(k\). Because non-manufacturing sectors are excluded, \(S_\ell = \sum_k s_{\ell k}\) is the commuting zone’s overall manufacturing share, which ranges from near 0% to over 40%.

Meanwhile, Chinese exports to high-income countries grew in almost every manufacturing industry, so \(\mu = \Er[g_k] > 0\). Consequently, \(Z_\ell = \mu S_\ell + \sum_k s_{\ell k}(g_k - \mu)\) is mechanically higher in manufacturing-heavy commuting zones! If manufacturing-heavy commuting zones were already experiencing secular employment declines unrelated to China, omitting \(S_\ell\) from the controls \(W_\ell\) creates severe omitted variable bias.

Controlling for \(S_\ell\) (or \(S_{\ell t}\) interacted with time dummies in a stacked-differences panel) restores orthogonality under exogenous shocks, because it isolates the variation in \(Z_\ell\) coming from which manufacturing industries a region specializes in, rather than how much manufacturing it has overall.

Pitfall 2: Non-Random Exposure to Exogenous Shocks

  • Generalization of incomplete shares (Borusyak and Hull (2023)):
    • Even when shocks \(g = (g_1, \dots, g_K)\) are exogenous, expected exposure \[ \mu_\ell(s) = \Er[Z_\ell \mid s] = \sum_{k=1}^K s_{\ell k} \Er[g_k \mid s] \] can vary across units \(\ell\) and correlate with \(u_\ell\)
  • Example: Regions near borders or ports, or central nodes in a network, have systematically higher expected exposure regardless of which shock realizes
  • Fix (Recentering): Subtract expected exposure from the instrument: \[ \tilde{Z}_\ell = Z_\ell - \mu_\ell(s) \] or control flexibly for \(\mu_\ell(s)\) (computed by permuting/simulating counterfactual shocks)

Borusyak and Hull (2023, Econometrica) generalize the incomplete-shares insight to any formula that combines exogenous shocks with nonrandom exposure—including shift-share instruments, network spillovers, and simulated policy instruments.

Suppose Chinese import growth is systematically larger in certain broad subsectors (say, low-skill labor-intensive goods), and some regions have higher exposure to those subsectors. Even if the exact industry-level shocks \(g_k\) are quasi-random around their subsector means, the expected value \(\Er[Z_\ell \mid s]\) varies across regions.

The fix is simple and powerful: recentering. Specify a counterfactual distribution of shocks (for example, permuting shocks within broad industry groups, or demeaning within groups), compute the average simulated instrument \(\mu_\ell(s) = \Er[Z_\ell \mid s]\) across counterfactual shock draws, and use \(\tilde{Z}_\ell = Z_\ell - \mu_\ell(s)\) as your instrument (or include \(\mu_\ell(s)\) as a control). Notice that controlling for \(S_\ell\) in Pitfall 1 is just a special case where \(\Er[g_k \mid s] = \mu\) is constant, so \(\mu_\ell(s) = \mu S_\ell\).

Pitfall 3: Inference & Cross-Regional Correlation

  • Conventional (heteroskedasticity-robust or geographically clustered) SEs assume \(u_\ell\) is independent across regions
  • Problem (Adão et al. (2019)): Regions with similar sectoral shares \(s_{\ell k}\) share common unobserved sectoral shocks in \(u_\ell\), inducing correlation across \(\ell\) regardless of geographic distance
    • In placebo exercises with randomly generated \(g_k\), conventional 5% tests reject up to 55% of the time!
  • Fixes:
    • AKM SEs (Adão et al. (2019)): exposure-robust variance estimator accounting for cross-regional correlation in residuals
    • Shock-level SEs (Borusyak et al. (2022)): run the equivalent shock-level IV regression and cluster SEs at the shock (or broader sector) level

This is the main result of Adão, Kolesár, and Morales (2019, QJE). Suppose we regress regional wage changes on a shift-share variable using purely random, simulated industry shocks \(g_k\). Under the null hypothesis of no effect, a 5% test should reject 5% of the time. Yet AKM show that using standard Eicker-Huber-White SEs or state-clustered SEs rejects the null up to 55% of the time!

Why? Because the regression residual \(u_\ell\) itself contains unobserved industry-level shocks (say, domestic automation or demand shifts in textiles). Two commuting zones—say, one in North Carolina and one in Maine—that both have large textile shares will have correlated residuals \(u_\ell\) and correlated instruments \(Z_\ell\), even though they are in different states! Standard geographic clustering misses this completely.

Both AKM (2019) and Borusyak, Hull, and Jaravel (2022) provide valid inference. AKM derive an analytical exposure-robust standard error formula in the region-level regression. BHJ show that when you transform the data to the shock level, standard heteroskedasticity-robust (or sector-clustered) SEs in the shock-level regression automatically yield valid asymptotic inference! In practice, BHJ’s shock-level SEs and AKM’s SEs are closely related, and both are much more reliable than conventional region-level SEs.

Pitfall 4: Serial Correlation & Dynamic Adjustment

  • In panel / stacked-differences settings (especially immigration, Jaeger et al. (2018)):
    • Shares \(s_{\ell k}\) (e.g., ethnic enclaves) are highly persistent over time
    • Shocks \(g_{kt}\) (e.g., origin-country migration waves) are serially correlated
  • Consequence: \(Z_{\ell t}\) is correlated with past shocks \(Z_{\ell, t-1}\)
  • If outcomes adjust slowly (e.g., native out-migration, capital reallocation), \(\hat{\beta}_{SSIV}\) conflates short-run impact and long-run adjustment
  • Fix: Include lagged treatment \(X_{\ell, t-1}\) instrumented by lagged shift-share \(Z_{\ell, t-1}\) (multiple instrumentation) when shocks have independent variation over time

Jaeger, Ruist, and Stuhler (2018) highlight a subtle dynamic pitfall that is especially severe in immigration shift-share designs like Card (2001).

In the U.S., the national mix of immigrants by origin country changes slowly across decades, and historical settlement shares are very persistent. As a result, the shift-share instrument in the 1990s is strongly correlated with the shift-share instrument in the 1980s. If an immigration wave in the 1980s depressed local wages in the short run, and native workers or firms gradually adjusted during the 1990s (pushing wages back up), then a standard 1990s shift-share regression picks up both the negative short-run effect of 1990s arrivals and the positive mean-reverting adjustment to 1980s arrivals!

Their proposed solution is to include both current and lagged inflows \((X_{\ell t}, X_{\ell, t-1})\) in the regression, instrumented by \((Z_{\ell t}, Z_{\ell, t-1})\). This works if there is enough change in the origin-country composition \(g_{kt}\) over time so that \(Z_{\ell t}\) and \(Z_{\ell, t-1}\) are not collinear.

Pitfall 5: Concentration & Weak Effective Sample Size

  • Under exogenous shocks: Consistency requires \(\sum_{k=1}^K s_k^2 \to 0\)
    • Effective number of shocks is \(1 / \sum_k s_k^2\) (inverse Herfindahl index of sector exposures)
    • Even if \(L = 722\) regions, if 3 industries account for 80% of exposure, you effectively have \(N_{\text{eff}} \approx 3\) observations!
  • Under exogenous shares: Rotemberg weights often reveal extreme concentration
    • Goldsmith-Pinkham et al. (2020) show that in many classic Bartik applications, the top 5 industries carry over half of the total Rotemberg weight \(\sum \hat{\alpha}_k\)
    • Some \(\hat{\alpha}_k\) can even be negative, complicating causal interpretation under heterogeneous effects

A deceptive feature of shift-share designs is that the region-level sample size \(L\) (e.g., 722 commuting zones or 3,000 counties) can look huge, giving a false sense of precision and power.

Under shock-based identification, your true sample size is governed by the shocks, not the regions! Specifically, Borusyak, Hull, and Jaravel (2022) recommend reporting the inverse Herfindahl index of exposure weights, \(1 / \sum_k s_k^2\), along with the largest sector shares \(\max_k s_k\). If the effective number of shocks is small—as in Card (2001) when a few origin countries dominate—shock-based asymptotics and shock-level SEs are unreliable.

Under share-based identification, Goldsmith-Pinkham, Sorkin, and Swift (2020) show that looking at the top Rotemberg weights \(\hat{\alpha}_k\) is equally eye-opening: you often discover that your entire 2SLS estimate is essentially driven by 2 or 3 specific industries (or 1–2 origin countries). Knowing which sectors drive the estimate lets you scrutinize those specific sectors for confounding pre-trends.

Recommendations for Practice

Practical Checklist (Borusyak et al. (2025))

  1. Declare your identification strategy: Are you relying on exogenous shocks or exogenous shares?
  2. If relying on exogenous shocks:
    • Control for sum of shares \(S_\ell\) (incomplete shares) and recenter if exposure is nonrandom (Borusyak and Hull (2023))
    • Report effective number of shocks (\(1/\sum_k s_k^2\)) and largest shock weights
    • Run shock-level balance tests and shock-level IV regressions (ssaggregate)
    • Use exposure-robust SEs (Adão et al. (2019)) or shock-level clustered SEs (Borusyak et al. (2022))
  3. If relying on exogenous shares:
    • Compute Rotemberg weights \(\hat{\alpha}_k\) (Goldsmith-Pinkham et al. (2020)) and inspect the top-weighted sectors
    • Test balance and pre-trends for the highest-weighted shares
    • Check sensitivity / overidentification across shares
  4. In dynamic panels: Check serial correlation of \(g_{kt}\) and \(Z_{\ell t}\); control for lagged shocks if needed (Jaeger et al. (2018))

To wrap up, here is a concrete checklist drawn from Borusyak, Hull, and Jaravel (2025) and the broader methodological literature.

First, never treat a shift-share instrument as a “black box.” Be explicit in your paper about whether your identification argument rests on quasi-random shocks (many independent \(g_k\)) or quasi-random shares (exogenous \(s_{\ell k}\)).

Second, if you are in the exogenous-shocks camp (like Autor-Dorn-Hanson): always check whether shares sum to 1, and if not, control for \(S_\ell\) (or recenter via counterfactual shocks). Aggregate to the shock level to check balance on pre-determined shock characteristics and to compute the effective number of shocks. And never report only conventional region-level SEs—use AKM or shock-level SEs.

Third, if you are in the exogenous-shares camp (more plausible when \(K\) is small): compute Rotemberg weights to see which sectors are doing the heavy lifting, and run balance/pre-trend checks specifically on those top-weighted shares.

Finally, in multi-period panels, check whether lagged shocks are confounding short-run and long-run effects.
